30r Chamber Conjecture

Start with a prime that sits immediately before a multiple of thirty. Look at the whole numbers between that prime and the next prime, and count the positive divisors of each one. Choose the first number with the smallest count.

The multiple of thirty usually loses. It already has the factors 2, 3, and 5, while a neighbor usually has fewer divisors. But there are chambers in which every neighbor has at least as many divisors. Then the multiple of thirty wins.

The conjecture says that every such win, apart from the one-number chamber between twin primes, has the same shape: the next prime is eight steps away, the winning number has sixteen divisors, and at least one later number ties it.

A row of divisor counts inside the gap would therefore force a precise location for its closing prime. That connection between the interior and the endpoint is the central question.

A chamber you can inspect

The first measured example starts at 17,666,309. Its next prime is 17,666,317. The number immediately after the first prime is 17,666,310 = 30 × 588,877.

The factors differ from chamber to chamber. The conjecture concerns the shared arrangement of divisor counts and the location of the endpoint.

The precise statement

Let p < q be consecutive primes with p > 5 and p ≡ 29 (mod 30). Write p = 30r − 1, where r is a positive integer. Let τ(n) count the positive divisors of n.

The interior is I = {p+1, …, q−1}. Its selected witness is the smallest integer attaining the minimum divisor count:

w = min { n ∈ I : τ(n) = minm ∈ I τ(m) }.

This is the leftmost minimum-divisor selection used by GWR.

30r Chamber Conjecture.

If q > p+2 and w = p+1, then

q − p = 8,   τ(w) = 16,   and   ∃ n ∈ {p+2, …, q−1} with τ(n) = 16.

The first hypothesis excludes twin primes: when q=p+2, the interior contains a single integer, so it wins automatically. The conjecture asserts that every remaining win has all three properties. One counterexample to any property disproves the combined statement.

What arithmetic already forces

Eight is the first available distance after two

Because the left prime is 30r−1, the integers four and six steps after it are composite:

p+4 = 3(10r+1),    p+6 = 5(6r+1).

Both factors in each product exceed one. Odd distances also land on even integers greater than two. The admissible even distances begin 2, 8, 12, 14, 18, 20, 24, 30. These residue restrictions permit a prime endpoint; they do not guarantee one.

Sixteen divisors identify the prime cofactor at these magnitudes

Write a multiple of thirty as n=2a3b5cm, with a,b,c≥1 and m having no factors 2, 3, or 5. Then

τ(n) = (a+1)(b+1)(c+1)τ(m).

If this product equals sixteen and m>1, each of the four factors is at least two, so all four equal two. Thus a=b=c=1 and m is prime. If m=1, the three factors must be 4, 2, 2 in some order, giving exactly 120, 270, or 750.

Every multiple of thirty above 750 with sixteen divisors is therefore thirty times a prime greater than five. In the measured chambers, the prime cofactor follows from the divisor count; it is not an additional independent pattern.

Similarly, a multiple of thirty with fewer than sixteen divisors must be one of 30, 60, 90, or 150. Sixteen is consequently the lowest possible divisor count for multiples of thirty above 150.

The two interior positions that cannot both tie

When r is odd, the integers p+3=30r+2 and p+7=30r+6 are consecutive multiples of four. Exactly one is divisible by four but not eight. Writing that integer as 4m with odd m gives τ(4m)=3τ(m). Its count cannot be sixteen.

This excludes simultaneous sixteen-count ties at those two positions. In a winning count-16 chamber, the restricted position must have at least eighteen divisors. The rule explains the absence of a final-position tie in seventeen of the original twenty winners; the other three remain unexplained by it.

This is an exact elementary deduction for odd r. The stronger observation that every winning chamber has τ(p+7)>16 is kept separate from the three-clause conjecture.

The ordered pattern and its losing neighbors

For a gap of eight, the first integer wins exactly when all six later counts are at least its count. That condition defines the selection; it does not explain which count sequences arithmetic allows.

Proof status and the next decisive test

The conjecture remains unresolved. The exact residue exclusions, cofactor classification, and two-position restriction above have explicit arithmetic derivations. They do not yet imply the three conjectured conclusions.

The universal GWR and selected-witness offset theorems retain their status in PROOF.md. In particular, the proved ceiling

w − p ≤ max(64, ⌈½(log q)2⌉)

bounds the selected witness’s offset. A winner at p+1 already has offset one; that bound alone does not force the right endpoint to be p+8.

Next falsification test: in an unexamined band, collect every non-twin chamber with p ≡ 29 (mod 30) whose selected witness is p+1. Do not preselect gap length, winning divisor count, or multiplicity. Test all three conclusions afterward, and retain any counterexample with its complete ordered divisor-count field.
